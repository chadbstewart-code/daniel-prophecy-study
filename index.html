How to use this study

The graphics below are arranged by the biblical chapters they emphasize. Begin with Daniel 2’s broad kingdom pattern, move to Daniel 7’s expanded vision of the beasts and judgment, then examine the ten-horn transition and Daniel 11’s more detailed conflict sequence.

What the scripture says
These observations come directly from the text: the kingdoms, beasts, ten horns, little horn, Ancient of Days, judgment, and the stone kingdom.
Interpretive framework
Modern nation identifications, the “nesting-doll” replay, and some proposed event correlations are interpretive. They should be examined against the scriptures rather than treated as explicit identifications made by Daniel.
1 • Foundation

Daniel 2 — The Stone and the Kingdom That Endures

Daniel 2 gives the broad architecture. Nebuchadnezzar sees a great image composed of successive materials. Daniel identifies the head of gold with Babylon and describes kingdoms that follow, ending in a divided iron-and-clay order. The vision then shifts from human kingdoms to a kingdom established by God.

Read: Daniel 2:31–45, especially vv. 34–35 and 44–45.

Notice the harvest language. After the image is broken, its kingdoms become “like the chaff of the summer threshingfloors,” and the wind carries them away (Daniel 2:35). The vision therefore moves beyond military conquest to separation and removal, while the stone remains and fills the earth.

Joseph Smith’s reported millstone analogy

A useful Latter-day Saint interpretive lens pictures the stone not merely as a projectile, but as a kingdom that grows as people gather to it. In that reading, the emphasis is on expansion: the kingdom increases until the final worldly order can no longer coexist with it. The graphic uses a millstone shape to make that growth visible while retaining Daniel’s own language about the stone becoming a great mountain.

2 • Expansion of the pattern

Daniel 2 & Daniel 7 — The Kingdom Pattern Revisited

Daniel 7 revisits the succession of earthly powers through four beasts. The angel explicitly says that the four beasts represent four kings or kingdoms (Daniel 7:17, 23). The vision then lingers on the fourth beast, its ten horns, another horn arising among them, persecution of the Saints, and the heavenly court.

Read: Daniel 7:2–14, 17–27. Compare Daniel 2:31–45.

The third beast is a leopard with four heads and four wings (Daniel 7:6). The fourfold imagery can be compared with Daniel 8:8, 21–22 and Daniel 11:3–4, where Alexander’s realm is divided toward the four winds. The fourth beast is deliberately unlike the previous animals: it is dreadful, powerful, and possesses ten horns (Daniel 7:7).

The turning point is judicial. Daniel watches the horn prevail against the Saints “until the Ancient of days came” and judgment was given to the Saints (Daniel 7:21–22). The same sequence is repeated in vv. 25–27: persecution, judgment, removal of dominion, then the kingdom given to the Saints.

Adam-ondi-Ahman in Latter-day Saint scripture

Doctrine and Covenants 27:11 identifies Michael as Adam, “the ancient of days,” and Doctrine and Covenants 116:1 associates Adam-ondi-Ahman with the place where the Ancient of Days will sit. That provides a specifically Latter-day Saint framework for comparing Daniel 7’s heavenly court with Adam-ondi-Ahman. Daniel itself supplies the vast assembly: “ten thousand times ten thousand stood before him” (Daniel 7:10).

3 • The final divided order

The Ten Horns — From the Fourth Kingdom to the Stout Horn

Daniel does not describe ten geographic regions. He interprets the ten horns as ten kings arising from the fourth kingdom (Daniel 7:24). This study uses the phrase “Fellowship of Ten Horns / Ten Conspirators” as an interpretive label for those rulers acting within the final political order.

Another ruler arises among or after the ten. Daniel describes him as having eyes like a man and a mouth speaking great things; three horns are uprooted or subdued before him (Daniel 7:8, 20, 24). He speaks against the Most High, persecutes the Saints, and seeks to change times and laws (Daniel 7:25).

Keep the sequence in view: fourth kingdom → ten rulers → another ruler arises → three are subdued → persecution intensifies → the heavenly court sits → his dominion is removed → the Saints receive the kingdom (Daniel 7:23–27).
4 • Detailed conflict and parallel witnesses

Daniel 11 — The Chain of Events

Daniel 11 adds a much denser conflict narrative. The graphic below places that sequence beside Isaiah and Doctrine and Covenants, and then beside Nephi and John, to ask whether multiple prophetic records illuminate different sides of a similar latter-day crisis.

Primary Daniel focus: Daniel 11:21–45. Related comparison passages are identified inside the graphic.

Daniel’s conflict

The proposed reading follows the ruler from his rise and covenant-making through conflict, persecution, desecration, and final actions. The classroom question is not merely “Who is this?” but also “What textual markers does Daniel give us for recognizing the sequence?” Particular attention belongs on Daniel 11:31–35 and 36–45.

Isaiah and latter-day revelation

Isaiah’s Assyrian passages can be read as a complementary adversary-and-deliverance pattern. Doctrine and Covenants should be used selectively: it strongly complements themes such as fleeing Babylon, gathering to Zion, the return of Israel, resurrection, judgment, and later appearances of Christ, but it should not be forced into every stage of Isaiah’s Assyrian narrative.

Nephi and John

Nephi provides an unusual textual handoff. After seeing the latter-day conflict and the power of the Lamb upon the covenant people, he sees one of the Twelve and is told that this apostle will write “the remainder of these things” (1 Nephi 14:20–27). The angel identifies that writer with John. This makes Revelation especially valuable as a companion text rather than merely a coincidental parallel.

Bringing the chapters together

Daniel 2 supplies the broad pattern of kingdoms and the enduring stone kingdom. Daniel 7 revisits the kingdom sequence and opens the final transition: ten rulers, the persecuting horn, the Ancient of Days, judgment, and the transfer of dominion. Daniel 11 supplies a more detailed conflict narrative that can then be compared with other prophetic witnesses.

The central classroom theme is therefore larger than identifying modern nations: earthly dominion is temporary, God’s judgment is decisive, covenant Israel is gathered, and the kingdom God establishes ultimately endures.

Questions for class discussion

  1. What does Daniel 2 tell us that Daniel 7 does not—and what does Daniel 7 add?
  2. Why might Daniel use both political imagery (kingdoms, horns, rulers) and agricultural imagery (chaff, threshing, gathering)?
  3. What changes in Daniel 7 when the scene moves from the beast and horn to the Ancient of Days?
  4. Which parts of the proposed latter-day framework are explicit in scripture, and which depend on interpretation?
  5. How does 1 Nephi 14’s handoff to John change the way we read Revelation alongside the Book of Mormon?
  6. What does the image of a kingdom that grows until it fills the earth teach about discipleship now?
